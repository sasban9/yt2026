There is a real tension here, but I would separate two different issues:

how language naturally evolves, and whether institutions should deliberately change language for social reasons.

Language was never designed by committee for maximum precision

Language is primarily a tool for communication, not a formal specification language.

English constantly trades precision for convenience:

  • “I’ll see you later.”
  • “It’s cold.”
  • “That’s expensive.”
  • “They said it was fine.”

All of these require context. We tolerate ambiguity because making every sentence perfectly explicit would make ordinary communication painfully cumbersome.

So the existence of ambiguity isn't necessarily a failure of language.

But your criticism becomes stronger when ambiguity is deliberately introduced

There is a meaningful difference between:

Language naturally developing new forms.

and:

Institutions expecting everyone to adopt a new linguistic convention because an existing convention is considered socially problematic.

The second can absolutely create friction.

For example, if a conventional grammatical structure historically communicated information efficiently,

replacing it with something that requires the listener to infer the intended meaning can reduce clarity

— particularly in:
  • legal documents
  • medicine
  • engineering
  • aviation
  • technical documentation
  • education
  • computer interfaces

In those contexts, precision should generally win over ideological elegance.

The interesting part is that language usually doesn't obey the majority anyway

This is where the premise gets complicated.

English grammar hasn't historically been controlled by "the majority." It evolves through millions of individual choices.

A form initially used by a minority can eventually become standard because enough people find it useful.

Consider:

  • “You” replacing “thou”
  • singular “they”
  • contractions such as “don't”
  • informal verbs becoming standard usage
  • words changing meaning over centuries

At some point, what was perceived as "incorrect" becomes ordinary English.

So a minority introducing a linguistic change isn't inherently unusual. That's essentially how language evolves.

The legitimate question is whether people are being asked or forced

There's an important distinction between:

"Some people prefer using X."

and

"Everyone must use X, and refusing to do so is unacceptable."

The first is ordinary linguistic evolution.

The second raises a legitimate question about institutional authority and compelled speech.

And there is an equally important distinction between:

"You don't have to use my preferred terminology."

and

"You are forbidden from using the traditional terminology."

The latter deserves considerably more scrutiny.

Precision isn't always the same thing as traditional grammar

This is perhaps the strongest counterpoint to your argument.

Suppose a new grammatical convention actually makes a previously ambiguous sentence less ambiguous.

Then changing grammar could increase precision rather than decrease it.

Likewise, some traditional constructions are surprisingly ambiguous:

“John told David that he was late.”

Who was late?

John or David?

Natural language has enormous amounts of ambiguity despite centuries of established grammar.

So we shouldn't automatically assume:

old grammar = precise
new grammar = vague

The real test should be:

Does the convention allow the listener to recover the intended meaning more reliably and with less effort?

There's also a hidden cost:
cognitive overhead

Your "guessing game" point is particularly relevant here.

Every unfamiliar linguistic convention imposes a temporary processing cost.

If a speaker uses terminology that the listener hasn't encountered before, the listener has to perform:

words → interpretation → intended meaning

rather than simply:

words → meaning

That matters enormously when communicating with millions of people.

A language convention becomes successful when its benefits eventually outweigh that cognitive cost.

The principle I'd use

Rather than asking:

Should the majority change its grammar for the minority?

I'd ask:

Does this linguistic change improve communication enough to justify the additional complexity it introduces?

That's a much harder—and more useful—standard.

If it improves clarity, adoption makes sense.

If it merely signals allegiance to a social position while making communication harder, resistance is understandable.

And if different groups can use different conventions without preventing communication, linguistic pluralism is probably preferable to forcing everyone into one system.

The fascinating thing is that English is currently undergoing exactly this kind of experiment in real time:

some innovations will become completely unremarkable in 20 years, while others will disappear because speakers simply won't find them useful.
